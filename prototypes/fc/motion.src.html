▢ faq
/faq · 3 questions

Before we launch

Will it slow the site down?
+

No. Animations use transforms, pause off-screen and are tested on mid-range phones.

Can you add motion to an existing Webflow site?
+

Yes, as long as the structure is clean. I review the build first and flag anything that needs tidying.

What about accessibility?
+

Every effect respects the reduced-motion setting and never hides content behind an animation.

How to add structured data to Webflow CMS pages (no plugin)
Paste a JSON-LD `<script>` block into the CMS template's Page settings › Custom code › Before `</head>`, and insert the item's fields with + Add field wherever a value belongs. Every item then ships its own structured data, built from the same fields as the page. Keep straight double quotes (`"`) out of those fields, because one of them breaks the whole block.

Your Webflow project pages might be invisible to Google. Check your links.
If JavaScript adds your cards' links, the HTML Google downloads may not link to your project pages at all. View the page source (not the inspector) and search for `/work/` or your collection's path. If nothing comes up, set each card's link to Current [Collection] page in the Designer so the link exists before any script runs.

Smooth scroll without the stutter: Lenis + GSAP in Webflow
Most Lenis + GSAP stutter in Webflow comes from three things: two animation clocks running at once, Lenis's stylesheet missing, and canvas animations that never pause. Drive Lenis from `gsap.ticker`, add Lenis's CSS, and stop anything that isn't on screen.

Interactive 3D in Webflow that doesn't wreck your mobile score
Heavy 3D doesn't have to mean a slow page. Load the scene only when it's about to be seen, pause it whenever it's off screen, render at a lower pixel ratio on touch devices, and give reduced-motion visitors a still image. The first screen stays fast because the 3D isn't on the critical path at all.

Tap is not hover: the touch bug hiding in interactive Webflow builds
On a touchscreen, a single tap fires `pointerenter`, `pointerleave` and then `click`, one after another. If hover code opens something on enter and closes it on leave, it closes it again before your click handler runs. Ignore touch in the hover handlers (`if (e.pointerType === 'touch') return;`) and let taps use `click` alone.

Versioned custom code for Webflow: GitHub, jsDelivr and SRI
Write your custom code in a GitHub repo, tag every release (`v1.4.0`), and load it in Webflow from jsDelivr pinned to that tag with an `integrity` hash. Every deploy is a new URL, so there's no cache to fight, a bad release rolls back by changing one version number, and the browser refuses a file that doesn't match its hash.

Reduced motion without losing the magic
When a visitor's device asks for reduced motion, keep everything the motion was saying and drop only the movement. Check `prefers-reduced-motion` in every animation, replace big moves with a fade or a still end state, and design the still version on purpose rather than just switching things off.

Why before how: what a philosophy degree taught me about building websites
The most useful thing philosophy taught me isn't any single idea. It's the habit of asking what something is for before deciding how to build it. In web projects, that one question saves more time than any tool: it turns "we need a new website" into a clear goal, a shorter sitemap and a design that has a reason for every section.

Discovery
One hour there, seven years here: Interstellar and why waiting feels longer than it is
In Interstellar, an hour on Miller's planet costs seven years back home, because a huge mass nearby really does slow time down relative to a distant observer. On the web, time bends in a different way: a wait with nothing to look at feels longer than the same wait with visible progress. You can't change physics, but you can change how a wait is experienced, by showing something useful immediately and making progress visible.

The camel, the lion and the child: Nietzsche on scope creep
In Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Nietzsche describes the spirit changing three times: into a camel that carries every burden, a lion that says "no" to the rules it was handed, and a child that creates something new. A good project goes through the same three stages. First you take everything in, then you refuse what doesn't serve the goal, and only then do you build. Skip the lion and you get scope creep.

Plato's cave: what a crawler sees is not what your visitors see
In Plato's cave, prisoners mistake shadows on a wall for the real world. A crawler reading your site is in a similar spot: it often sees only the raw HTML the server sends, not the page your visitors get after scripts run. If your links, titles or content only appear after JavaScript, some machines are reading the shadow. The fix is to put the real thing in the HTML itself.

The Ship of Theseus: when does a redesign stop being the same brand?
The ancient puzzle of the Ship of Theseus asks whether a ship is still the same ship once every plank has been replaced. Brands live that puzzle every time they refresh. My answer, from designing this site's own identity: a brand stays itself as long as a few deliberate things stay fixed (its proportions, its core colors, its rules about how pieces fit). Everything else can be replaced, plank by plank, without anyone feeling a stranger has moved in.

Amor fati: Nietzsche and learning to love Webflow's limits
Nietzsche called it amor fati, "love of fate": wanting nothing to be different, and not merely putting up with what is necessary but loving it. Every platform has necessities you can't argue with. On this site, Webflow's hard limits (a 60-field cap, nested lists that stop at five items, components that can't hold CMS lists) each forced a structure that turned out cleaner than what I'd planned. The limits weren't in the way of the design. They were part of it.

Descartes' method of doubt: publish, then check
Descartes built his philosophy on one rule: accept nothing as true unless you've seen for yourself that it is. On a website, that means a change isn't done when a tool says "saved" or "published". It's done when you've checked the live page and found it there. Descartes' four rules of method (verify, divide, order, enumerate) turn out to be a very good checklist for shipping a site.

Descartes' coordinates and a star chart of ideas
Descartes' great mathematical idea was to give every point a pair of numbers, an x and a y, so that shapes could be described with algebra and equations could be drawn as shapes. The star chart on the Topics page does something similar with ideas: each topic in this site's vocabulary gets a position, a size and lines to its neighbors, so you can see how the ideas connect instead of reading a list. A map makes relationships visible that a list hides.
